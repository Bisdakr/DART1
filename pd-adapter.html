Sterile & Aseptic Technique — the entire PD safety story
Why this matters before we touch a single connector

Peritonitis in PD is overwhelmingly a touch-contamination event. It doesn't come from cosmic causes; it comes from a hand on a hub, a mask pulled down to talk, an open door, a sneeze, a phone. The CDC, ISPD (International Society for Peritoneal Dialysis), and Baxter's HomeChoice Pro IFU all agree on the same point: the technique around the connection matters more than the connection itself. Set the room and yourself up first; the plastic will cooperate.

🔑
The rule that explains every other rule Treat every exposed luer like an open IV catheter into the peritoneum. Because functionally, that's what it is. Once a connection is open, the patient's peritoneal membrane is the next surface the air touches.

1 · Set the room before you set the field

🚪
Doors Closed
Eliminate cross-traffic and airflow. No "just running in real quick."
🌀
Fans / AC Off
Any directed airflow over the field carries skin flora. Turn it off for the duration of the exchange.
🐾
Pets Out
Dander, hair, dust — all out. Including birds and reptiles per ISPD guidance.
📱
Phones Away
A phone is a fomite. Pocket it once gloves go on — no texting mid-connection.
🧴
Clean Surface
Wipe the work surface with hospital-grade disinfectant. Let it air-dry per IFU contact time.
💡
Good Lighting
If you can't see the threads, you can't verify they're clean. Bright, direct light only.

2 · Then set yourself

😷
Mask On (Patient + Nurse)
Surgical mask, covering nose and mouth, before the cassette is opened. Replace if damp.
💧
Hand Hygiene — 20 sec
Soap-and-water wash, all surfaces, ≥ 20 sec. Dry with clean disposable towel. Turn off faucet with the towel.
🧤
Clean Gloves
Don after hand hygiene — never reuse. Some institutions specify sterile gloves for the actual connection step; follow your local SOP.
👤
No Talking, No Coughing
Once luers are exposed, mouths stay closed. Step away from the field if you must speak or sneeze.
💍
Bare Below the Elbows
Rings (plain band only or removed), watch, bracelets off. Long sleeves rolled or removed.
🪒
No Smoking, No Eating
Not during, not just before. Skin contaminants travel.
⚠
If you're sick — call out An upper respiratory infection, productive cough, or open sore on the hands means someone else does the connection today. PD nursing is a contact discipline; nobody wins by powering through.
The Connection — step by step
Baxter HomeChoice Pro · Fresenius transfer set · adapter bridge

Read this once cold before doing it warm. Each step is one motion, one verification. The "boring" details (which way the cap comes off, where you set the cycler tubing while you pick up the adapter) are the steps that protect the patient. Verify every clamp; verify every cap; verify every connection.

  1. Verify the order and the prescription. Confirm patient identity, prescription, dialysate strength, target volumes, dwell time, and number of cycles. Match against the HomeChoice Pro program card. Mismatch caught here is one minute saved. Mismatch caught at fill time is a sentinel event.
  2. Gather and inspect the disposables. Open the cycler set, the dialysate bags, and the Fresenius–Baxter adapter package. Check expiration dates, lot numbers, package integrity, and clarity of dialysate (no particulate, no haziness, no color change). Reject anything that looks off — document and replace.
  3. Stage components without touching luers. Place the cycler tubing, the adapter (still capped), and the patient's transfer set (still capped, still clamped) on the clean field. Keep caps on until you need to remove them. No premature unwrapping.
  4. Hand hygiene + mask check. Soap-and-water wash, ≥ 20 seconds, all surfaces. Dry. Confirm both patient and nurse masks are on, snug, covering nose and mouth. Patient turns the head away from the field.
  5. Don clean gloves. Do not reach below the field, do not touch face, do not adjust hair. Hands stay in the working space.
  6. Verify the patient's transfer set is clamped. Slide-clamp on the patient transfer set — closed. Visually confirm. If the patient line is unclamped during the swap, dialysate or air can move uncontrolled.
  7. Prime the cycler per Baxter HomeChoice Pro IFU. Load the cassette, hang the dialysate bags, run the prime cycle. Verify priming completes without alarms. Verify the warmer is functioning and the cycler tubing is clear of air. Never connect to the patient until prime is verified complete.
  8. Final luer check — three components, three caps. Cycler tubing end-cap, adapter both ends, patient transfer set end-cap. All present, none touched.
  9. Remove the cap from the cycler tubing. Pull straight off — don't twist a thread. Hold the connector by the body, not the luer. Once exposed, the luer is sterile — do not let it touch any surface, including your gloved hand.
  10. Remove the corresponding cap from the adapter. Same technique. Connect the cycler tubing to the adapter using a smooth, deliberate motion — straight on, gentle twist to seat the luer-lock. Verify a snug seal; no wobble. No re-do — if either luer touches anything, discard and start that component over.
  11. Remove the opposite cap from the adapter, and the cap from the patient transfer set. Connect the adapter to the patient transfer set. Same straight-on, no-twist-then-twist motion. Confirm full seating.
  12. Verify the entire chain. Cycler → adapter → patient transfer set. Trace the path with your eye. Look for kinks, twists, or strain. If anything looks off, stop and assess before opening a clamp.
  13. Open the patient's transfer-set clamp. Open in the correct direction per IFU. Confirm the cycler reads inflow / outflow pressures within range. If alarms or pressure issues fire, stop the cycle, close the patient clamp, and troubleshoot before re-opening.
  14. Begin therapy. Confirm the HomeChoice Pro is running the prescribed program. Monitor the first cycle closely. Stay in the room for the first inflow.
  15. Document. Time of connection, lot numbers (cycler set, adapter, dialysate bags), nurse and patient identifiers, any alarms or interventions, dialysate clarity, exit-site appearance, and patient tolerance.
⊘
"If in doubt, swap it out" Any luer that touches a glove, the field, the patient's skin, the cycler casing — anything that is not its mating connector — is contaminated. Discard that component, open a new one, and start that step over. A 90-second restart is cheaper than a peritonitis admission.
⚠
Disconnection follows the same discipline At the end of the run, disconnection is connection in reverse. Re-mask, re-hand-hygiene, re-glove, clamp first, cap immediately. A capped luer is a safe luer; an unwrapped one is not.
After the connection — what to watch
Recognizing early peritonitis isn't optional

Even with perfect technique, peritonitis can happen. The earliest sign is almost always cloudy effluent — a turbidity that wasn't there at the start of the cycle. Other early flags: abdominal pain (often described as a constant ache rather than colic), low-grade fever, nausea, or a sudden drop in ultrafiltration. Drain a sample, send for cell count + differential + Gram stain + culture, notify the physician. Empiric intraperitoneal antibiotics are typically started within hours of suspicion per ISPD guidelines — don't wait for cultures to return.

The exit site and tunnel also deserve a daily look: erythema, drainage, tenderness, scab change. Catch a tunnel infection before it becomes peritonitis.

🔑
The mindset Sterile technique isn't a skill you "learn." It's a discipline you practice, every single connection, on every single patient, forever. The day it becomes routine is the day you re-check yourself harder.
